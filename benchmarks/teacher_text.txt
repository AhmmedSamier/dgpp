The city of Lyon sits at the confluence of two rivers, the Rhône and the Saône, in the east of France. It was founded by the Romans in 43 BC as Lugdunum and served for a time as the capital of Roman Gaul. Its position on the trade routes between the Mediterranean and northern Europe made it wealthy, and by the Renaissance it had become a centre of banking and of printing. The silk trade arrived in the sixteenth century and shaped the city for three hundred years: the tall houses of the Croix-Rousse district were built with high ceilings to hold the looms, and the covered passageways known as traboules allowed weavers to carry finished cloth between streets without exposing it to the rain.

Lyon is often described as the gastronomic capital of France. The claim rests on a long tradition of small family restaurants called bouchons, which serve dishes built around pork, offal, and the local wines of the Beaujolais and the northern Rhône. The cooks who ran these kitchens in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries were frequently women, and several of them became famous in their own right. In the second half of the twentieth century the chef Paul Bocuse, whose restaurant stood just north of the city, held three Michelin stars for more than fifty consecutive years, a record that helped fix the region's reputation.

The old town, on the west bank of the Saône below the hill of Fourvière, is one of the largest Renaissance districts in Europe and was placed on the UNESCO World Heritage list in 1998. Above it stands the basilica of Notre-Dame de Fourvière, completed in 1884 and visible from most of the city. On the hill behind the basilica are the remains of two Roman theatres, still used for concerts every summer. Across the river, the Presqu'île, the narrow peninsula between the two rivers, holds the city's principal squares, its opera house, and its main shopping streets.

Modern Lyon is the third largest city in France by population and the second largest metropolitan area. It has a long industrial history in textiles, chemicals, and pharmaceuticals, and it remains an important centre for biotechnology and for the film industry, which traces its origins to the Lumière brothers, who shot what is usually considered the first motion picture in the city in 1895. The metropolitan area is served by an international airport, two major railway stations, and a metro system with four lines. Every December the city holds the Fête des Lumières, a festival of light in which residents place candles in their windows and the public buildings are illuminated with projected images, drawing several million visitors over four nights.
